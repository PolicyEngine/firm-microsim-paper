  \begin{alertblock}{The problem: VAT as a tax \emph{notch}}
    The UK VAT registration threshold is a \hl{notch}, not a kink: once
    turnover crosses the threshold, \hl{20\% VAT is due on the firm's
    \emph{whole} turnover}, not just the excess.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Among the \hl{highest thresholds in the OECD}, sitting in a
            dense part of the firm-size distribution.
      \item \hl{Frozen at \pounds85{,}000} from Apr 2017 to Mar 2024 --- the
            longest nominal freeze in the tax's history --- then raised to
            \hl{\pounds90{,}000} in Apr 2024.
      \item Fiscal drag pushes ever more firms toward the threshold; firms
            \hl{bunch below it} and slow their growth as they approach.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{Contribution}
    An \hl{open, reproducible} firm-level microsimulation for costing VAT
    threshold reforms, released with its \hl{synthetic-data generator}.
    It prices three families of reform on a \emph{common static basis}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \hl{Level} --- move the threshold up or down.
      \item \hl{Shape} --- a graduated taper that phases VAT in.
      \item \hl{Rate} --- a reduced-rate band above the threshold.
    \end{itemize}
    Shape reforms have no single point of excess mass, so reduced-form
    bunching methods cannot evaluate them --- a structural model can.
  \end{block}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Method}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \hl{Synthetic micro-data}: draw published ONS firm counts by
            sector\,$\times$\,turnover band; assign inputs, value added
            $v=y-x$, and employment. The threshold is a generator
            parameter, so data can be regenerated under any counterfactual.
      \item \hl{Calibration}: firm weights $w_i=e^{\theta_i}$ fit by
            gradient descent to HMRC counts and net VAT liability by band
            and sector, and ONS employment totals.
      \item Result: \hl{2.94m records} weighted to \hl{$\approx$2.5m UK
            firms} at \hl{$\approx$90\% calibration accuracy}.
      \item Static cost: $R(T)=\sum_i w_i v_i\,\mathds{1}\{y_i\ge T\}$ ---
            revenue moves only via firms whose registration status flips.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{block}{Validation against HMRC}
    Costing the \hl{\pounds85k$\to$\pounds90k} (Apr 2024) increase gives
    \hl{$-$\pounds175m} in 2025--26 vs the Government's published
    \hl{$-$\pounds185m} --- agreement \hl{within \pounds10m}, sharing the
    same turning point (a small gain by 2028--29).
    \begin{figure}
      \centering

    \end{figure}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{The notch and its \emph{dominated region}}
    The notch's distortion is characterised exactly by the
    \hl{dominated region}: the band above the threshold,
    $a = T^\ast\,\tfrac{\tau}{1-\tau}$, in which \hl{no firm can profitably
    locate}. It is \hl{elasticity-free}.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Width \hl{$a=$\pounds21{,}250} (\pounds85{,}000--\pounds106{,}250)
            --- an order of magnitude wider than a kink's $\sim$\pounds1k.
      \item Spans \hl{$\approx$137{,}000} weighted firms; notch jump at the
            threshold $=\tau T^\ast=$ \hl{\pounds17{,}000}.
    \end{itemize}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.96\colwidth]{figures/dynamic_notch_fit_e017.png}
    \end{figure}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Bunching here is \emph{mechanical}}
    The estimated density step is a \hl{calibration artefact}, not behaviour
    --- a transparency lesson for synthetic data.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Estimator: bunching ratio $b=0.060$, excess mass
            \hl{8{,}712 firms}.
      \item \hl{Placebo} (remove the band-step target): collapses to
            \hl{$\approx$99 firms} --- the step was inherited from coarse
            HMRC bands.
    \end{itemize}
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.96\colwidth]{figures/bunching_analysis_85k.png}
    \end{figure}
  \end{block}

\begin{column}{\colwidth}

  \begin{alertblock}{Headline result}
    The three reforms are \hl{not interchangeable}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \hl{Raising the threshold} is the \emph{dearest} and removes
            \emph{no} distortion.
      \item A \hl{reduced rate} merely \emph{splits} the dominated band in two.
      \item \hl{Only the graduated taper removes the distortion entirely
            --- and for less} ($-$\pounds336m).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{Behavioural sensitivity}
    Reforms are also priced behaviourally for an assumed turnover elasticity
    $e$, swept over $\{0.05,0.17,0.32\}$. Because $e$ is \hl{assumed, not
    identified}, this is a disciplined \hl{sensitivity range} that
    \hl{nests the static cost} as $e\to0$.
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.96\colwidth]{figures/dynamic_cost_vs_elasticity.png}
    \end{figure}
    At $e=0.17$: raise-to-\pounds100k $-$\pounds292m (50{,}155 firms
    re-optimise); reduced-rate 10\% $-$\pounds273m; 15\% $-$\pounds135m.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Policy conclusions}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The \hl{static cost} and the \hl{exact dominated region} are
            the robust, elasticity-free anchors for comparing reforms.
      \item Behavioural magnitudes are a sensitivity band conditional on an
            unidentified $e$ --- not point estimates.
      \item \hl{Synthetic bunching must not be over-read} as behaviour.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Scope \& next steps}
    Taxes \emph{turnover} (one input): a turnover-tax-notch approximation for
    consumer-facing, limited-reclaim firms. Abstracts from input-VAT reclaim
    and voluntary registration. \hl{Chief next task}: identify $e$ from
    administrative micro-data to turn the range into a point estimate, plus a
    welfare / MVPF analysis.
  \end{exampleblock}
