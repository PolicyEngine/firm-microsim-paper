\section{Behavioural reform costs}
\label{sec:behavioural}

The reforms of Section~\ref{sec:static} are costed \emph{statically}: each
schedule is re-applied to a fixed turnover distribution, and the revenue change is
the mechanical reclassification of firms with no response in turnover.
Section~\ref{sec:bunching} then shows that the bunching in my synthetic data is
mechanical, so a behavioural elasticity is not identified from it---the reduced-form
response is a placebo. Section~\ref{sec:model} supplies the one object that needs
no behavioural calibration at all: the exact, elasticity-free dominated region. What is
missing from this sequence is the behavioural layer itself---a costing that lets
firms re-optimise when a reform changes the effective rate they face. This
section supplies that layer. I build an iso-elastic structural simulator that, for
an \emph{assumed} elasticity $e$, re-solves each firm's optimum under a
counterfactual schedule and prices the resulting revenue effect. Because $e$ is not
identified from these data, I do not report a point estimate; I report an
$e$-sensitivity range. The simulator nests the static costing of
Section~\ref{sec:static} as its $e\to0$ limit, which I use below as a correctness
check rather than treat as a coincidence. This is honest infrastructure, and every
number in it is explicitly conditional on $e$.

The reframing this section makes precise is that VAT is a tax on \emph{value added},
not on whole turnover. A registered firm charges output VAT on its sales but
reclaims input VAT on its purchases, so the base it is taxed on is its value added,
not its turnover. I therefore set out the firm's problem as a choice over value
added, with turnover following mechanically, and show that the operational simulator
that produces the costing below is---once the firm's deductible inputs are
accounted for---algebraically a value-added tax. This is the ``new setting'' in
which the reported numbers should be read: a value-added base, with the
turnover-tax notch of Section~\ref{sec:model} recovered as the limited-reclaim,
consumer-facing special case.

\subsection{An iso-elastic structural simulator}
\label{ssec:simulator}

I adopt the iso-elastic firm problem of \citet{klevenwaseem2013}, in the
sufficient-statistics tradition of \citet{saez2010} and as applied to UK VAT by
\citet{liuetal2021}. Let $\delta$ denote the firm's \emph{deductible-input share}
of turnover, so that a firm with turnover $y$ has value added $z=(1-\delta)\,y$ and
value-added share $1-\delta$. Because VAT is levied on value added, I take the firm
to choose its value added $z$ directly. A firm of ability $n$ then solves
\begin{equation}
  \pi(z;n) \;=\; \bigl(1-\tau\,f(z)\bigr)\,z \;-\;
  \frac{n}{1+1/e}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)^{1+1/e},
  \label{eq:isoelastic}
\end{equation}
where $f(z)\in\{0,1\}$ indicates registration under the schedule---so $\tau\,f(z)$
is the effective rate the schedule levies on value added---and $e>0$ is the
elasticity of value added with respect to the net-of-tax rate $1-\tau$. Marginal
cost is $(z/n)^{1/e}$, so the frictionless ($\tau=0$) optimum is $z=n$: ability $n$
is the firm's \emph{undistorted value added}. A firm registered under a flat rate
$\tau$ chooses
\begin{equation}
  z^{*} \;=\; n\,(1-\tau)^{e},
  \label{eq:flat-optimum}
\end{equation}
and one verifies directly from~\eqref{eq:flat-optimum} that
$\mathrm{d}\ln z / \mathrm{d}\ln(1-\tau) = e$, so $e$ is exactly the knob that
governs the intensive response. Turnover follows mechanically as
$y=z/(1-\delta)$, and the \pounds85{,}000 registration test, which is levied on
\emph{turnover}, maps to a sector-specific value-added threshold
$z_{T}=(1-\delta)\,T^{*}$.

This formulation---call it \emph{A2}, the value-added-choice formulation---has two
clean properties. First, ability is bounded: $n$ is a finite undistorted value
added, and the recovered abilities behave well across the population. Second, the
turnover dominated region is \emph{independent of $\delta$}. Registering drops a
firm's net value-added revenue at the threshold by $\tau\,z_{T}=\tau(1-\delta)T^{*}$;
equating the net position of a threshold firm with that of a registered firm at
turnover $T^{*}+a$ gives $(1-\tau)(1-\delta)(T^{*}+a)=(1-\delta)T^{*}$, in which the
value-added share $(1-\delta)$ cancels, so
\begin{equation}
  a \;=\; T^{*}\,\frac{\tau}{1-\tau} \;=\; \pounds21{,}250,
  \label{eq:va-dominated}
\end{equation}
exactly the elasticity-free width of Section~\ref{sec:model}: both the threshold and
the notch jump scale by $(1-\delta)$, so the dominated region measured in turnover is
the same regardless of the input share. This is the sense in which the value-added
base leaves the exact, $e$-free anchor of Section~\ref{ssec:dominated} intact.

\paragraph{Why A2 and not the input-subtraction formulation.} An alternative
formulation---call it \emph{A}---keeps turnover as the choice variable and subtracts
deductible inputs as a separate cost. Formulation A reproduces the same notch jump
at the threshold, $\tau(1-\delta)T^{*}$, but when one inverts observed turnover for
ability it recovers \emph{ill-behaved} abilities that diverge as $\delta$ rises,
because the input cost enters the first-order condition without rescaling the choice
variable. A2, by treating value added as the object the firm actually optimises over,
avoids this and recovers bounded abilities. The two formulations---and the
whole-turnover notch of Section~\ref{sec:model}---coincide at $\delta=0$, where value
added equals turnover; A2 is the principled formulation away from that corner, which
is why I adopt it.

\paragraph{Tying A2 to the reported costing.} The operational simulator does not
carry $\delta$ as a free object firm by firm; it prices each reform through the
firm's \emph{effective value-added rate} $\tau(1-\delta)$, applied as a turnover
wedge $\tau_i$. That wedge is the firm's net remittance over turnover---output VAT
less input credits---which averages about $3\%$ of turnover in the calibrated
population (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Pricing a reform through $\tau_i$ on turnover is
algebraically identical to levying $\tau$ on value added $z=(1-\delta)y$, since
$\tau_i\,y=\tau(1-\delta)y=\tau z$; the turnover wedge and the value-added tax are
the same object. This is precisely the machine that produces
Table~\ref{tab:behavioural_costs}. The sector value-added share $1-\delta$ is at
present \emph{parametric}: it is taken from the calibrated net-VAT population rather
than measured directly, and I flag that grounding the share $1-\delta$ in ONS
Supply--Use / Annual Business Survey gross-value-added-to-turnover data is left to
future work. For now the $\sim3\%$ effective wedge is what the simulator carries.

\paragraph{Ability recovery.} To populate the simulator I recover an ability $n$
for each firm from its observed turnover under the baseline \pounds85{,}000 notch.
Given $e$, I set $n=(1-\delta_i)\,y_{\mathrm{obs}}$ for an unregistered firm
($y_{\mathrm{obs}}<T^{*}$)---its undistorted value added---and
invert~\eqref{eq:flat-optimum} for a registered firm,
$n=(1-\delta_i)\,y_{\mathrm{obs}}/(1-\tau_i)^{e}$. The wedge $\tau_i$ here is the
firm's \emph{own} net VAT rate---net remittance over turnover, that is output VAT
less input credits, which averages about $3\%$ of turnover in the calibrated
population (Section~\ref{sec:data})---and \emph{not} the statutory $20\%$. This
distinction is material: VAT is levied on value added, not on turnover, so a firm
remitting $3\%$ of its turnover has been distorted as if it faced a net-of-tax rate
near $0.97$, not $0.80$. Inverting~\eqref{eq:flat-optimum} at the statutory $20\%$
would inflate the recovered ability gap, and hence every counterfactual response,
several-fold.

\paragraph{Forward solve.} Holding the recovered abilities $\{n_i\}$ and the
assumed $e$ fixed, I re-solve each firm's optimum under the reform schedule---the
raised threshold, the graduated taper, or the reduced-rate band---and aggregate the
resulting liabilities. The behavioural revenue effect is the difference between this
aggregate and the baseline. Because a reform that lowers the effective rate on the
band raises each affected firm's net-of-tax rate, the firm scales value added (and
hence turnover) up toward its frictionless optimum,
\begin{equation}
  y^{*}_i \;=\; y_{\mathrm{obs},i}\,
  \left(\frac{1-\tau_{\mathrm{reform}}}{1-\tau_{\mathrm{base}}}\right)^{e}
  \;>\; y_{\mathrm{obs},i},
  \label{eq:rescale}
\end{equation}
so the taxed base broadens and partly offsets the static loss.

\paragraph{Conditional on $e$.} I am explicit about what this exercise is and is
not. Treating each firm's observed (mechanically calibrated) turnover as optimal
and inverting it for $n$ is an \emph{accounting anchor}, not structural
identification: it reproduces the baseline by construction but borrows no
information about the response. The elasticity $e$ is not identified from these data
(the placebo of Section~\ref{sec:bunching}), so every behavioural magnitude below is
conditional on the assumed $e$. The robust, $e$-free headline objects remain the
static cost of Section~\ref{sec:static} and the exact dominated region of
Section~\ref{sec:model}; this section's numbers are a disciplined sensitivity range,
not point estimates. The margin priced here is purely \emph{intensive}---firms scale
value added as the effective rate moves. The extensive notch distortion, the discrete
incentive to suppress turnover and bunch below the threshold, is the separate
analytic object of Section~\ref{sec:model}; a reader should not expect re-bunching
dynamics here.

\subsection{Results}
\label{ssec:behavioural-results}

I sweep $e\in\{0.05,\,0.17,\,0.32\}$ and report the full sweep, treating
$e=0.17$ as an illustrative midpoint rather than a headline estimate. The low
value $0.05$ is of the order reported by \citet{klevenwaseem2013}; $0.32$ is the
mean implied by mapping the marginal-buncher condition through my notch; $0.17$ is
the median of that mapping. (These per-firm-mapping summaries are a different object
from the single aggregate marginal-buncher elasticity $\approx0.66$ tabulated in
Appendix~\ref{app:inference}, and differ from it numerically; both are mechanical,
non-identified artefacts of the calibration.) I flag the provenance plainly: the $0.17$ and $0.32$
values are read off the marginal-buncher condition applied to my notch geometry,
which inherits the mechanical bunching that Section~\ref{sec:bunching} shows to be
non-behavioural, so they are model-implied, not data-identified; the range's only
externally-anchored point is its lower end (the \citet{klevenwaseem2013} value of
$\approx 0.05$). I use them only to delimit a plausible sweep, and a reader
preferring a fully external range---for instance \citet{liuetal2021}'s UK VAT-base
elasticity---may substitute literature values for $e$ directly without changing the
conditional structure of the costing. The same mapping pins the marginal buncher
$n_H(e)$---the highest ability that still finds it optimal to bunch at the
threshold---at \pounds112{,}795 for $e=0.05$ (a frictionless excess
$\Delta y_{H}\equiv n_H-T^{*}\approx\pounds27.8$k, distinct from the empirical
bunching span $\Delta y^{*}=\pounds5.6$k of Appendix~\ref{app:inference}),
\pounds127{,}382 for $e=0.17$
($\approx\pounds42.4$k), and \pounds143{,}527 for $e=0.32$ ($\approx\pounds58.5$k),
consistent with the analytic dominated-region width $a=\pounds21{,}250$ of
Section~\ref{sec:model}.

Table~\ref{tab:behavioural_costs} reports the behavioural cost of each of the three
flat-rate reforms---the raised threshold and the two reduced-rate bands---measured
as the change in revenue relative to the \pounds85{,}000-notch baseline, alongside
the static figure of Table~\ref{tab:schedule_costs}, for each $e$. The direction is
uniform across these three: a larger $e$ makes each of them \emph{cheaper} in
revenue terms. The mechanism is~\eqref{eq:rescale}: each reform lowers the effective
rate on the band by a \emph{constant} amount, so firms scale value added up toward
their frictionless optimum, broadening the taxed base and partly offsetting the
static loss. The offset is substantial: at $e=0.17$ the raised threshold costs
\(-\)\pounds292m against a static \(-\)\pounds508m, and the $10\%$ and $15\%$ bands
cost \(-\)\pounds273m and \(-\)\pounds135m against static \(-\)\pounds343m and
\(-\)\pounds171m. At the illustrative midpoint $e=0.17$, the number of firms re-optimising is
$50{,}155$ under the raised threshold, $61{,}187$ under the $10\%$ reduced rate, and
$51{,}226$ under the $15\%$ reduced rate. Figure~\ref{fig:reform_dist} traces these
costs across the full range of $e$, with each curve nesting onto its static value as
$e\to0$.

\paragraph{Why the graduated taper is excluded.} I do not report a behavioural cost
for the graduated taper, and I want to be candid about why. The taper carries two
behavioural channels that pull in opposite directions. The first is the
base-broadening channel shared with the flat reforms: as the \emph{average} rate a
firm faces falls, the firm scales its value added up, which is cheaper for revenue in
exactly the way~\eqref{eq:rescale} captures. The second is a marginal-rate
distortion that is the taper's signature: because the taper's rate \emph{rises} with
turnover, raising output lifts the rate on \emph{all} of a firm's value added, so the
firm faces an incentive to hold turnover down---a force that is costlier for
revenue, not cheaper. The iso-elastic cost used here can price the first, upward
channel but not the second, downward one. Imposing the full marginal-rate wedge in a
correct first-order-condition solve makes firms collapse to the band floor and yields
economically pathological revenue losses of the order of several billion pounds---an
artefact of a cost function that cannot represent the downward, marginal-rate-driven
response a taper induces, not a credible policy estimate. The net behavioural sign of
the taper is therefore ambiguous and not identified in this framework. I take this as
an honest scope limit, consistent with the paper's careful identification stance, and
report only the taper's robust, $e$-free objects---its static cost
(Section~\ref{sec:static}) and its exact, analytic removal of the dominated region
(Section~\ref{sec:model}).

\paragraph{Validation: the $e\to0$ limit.} For the three flat-rate reforms the
simulator should reproduce the static costing as the response vanishes, and it does.
Taking $e\to0$ in the forward solve returns \(-\)\pounds507m for the raised
threshold, \(-\)\pounds343m for the $10\%$ reduced rate, and \(-\)\pounds171m for the
$15\%$ reduced rate---matching the static \(-\)\pounds508m, \(-\)\pounds343m, and
\(-\)\pounds171m of Table~\ref{tab:schedule_costs} to within about \pounds1m.
This is the intended nesting: when no firm responds, the behavioural cost collapses
to the mechanical reclassification effect, so the static costing is the $e=0$ corner
of the same machine, not a separate calculation. The nesting notably does \emph{not}
hold for the graduated taper---a further symptom that the iso-elastic cost cannot
represent the taper's marginal-rate channel, and an additional reason its behavioural
cost is not reported. I separately confirm numerically that
$\mathrm{d}\ln y^{*}/\mathrm{d}\ln(1-\tau)=e$ holds exactly in the solved population
for the flat reforms, so $e$ is indeed the governing knob.

\subsection{Heterogeneous registration incentives: input reclaim and B2C sales}
\label{ssec:liu-heterogeneity}

The single value-added wedge of Section~\ref{ssec:simulator} treats registration as
a uniform cost. That abstracts from two margins that \citet{liuetal2021} show matter
for who bunches and who does not. Registration brings an \emph{input-VAT reclaim
benefit} that rises in the firm's input share $\omega$---a registered firm recovers
the VAT on its purchases---and an \emph{output-VAT burden} that falls only on the
firm's business-to-consumer (B2C) sales, rising in the B2C share $\beta$, because VAT
charged on business-to-business (B2B) sales is reclaimed by the purchasing firm and
is not a net burden. To a first order the net effect of registering is therefore
proportional to
\begin{equation}
  t\,\bigl[\,\omega \;-\; \beta\,(1-\omega)\,\bigr],
  \label{eq:liu-net}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the VAT rate. The sign of~\eqref{eq:liu-net} flips across firm types.
For a consumer-facing, low-input firm ($\beta$ high, $\omega$ low) the bracket is
\emph{negative}: registering is a net cost, so the threshold is a downward notch and
the firm has an incentive to bunch below it. For an input-heavy B2B firm ($\omega$
high, $\beta$ low) the bracket is \emph{positive}: registering is a net gain through
input reclaim, so the firm registers voluntarily even below the threshold---which is
why roughly $43\%$ of eligible below-threshold UK firms choose to register
\citep{liuetal2021}. The single-wedge model prices the first type and is silent on
the second.

This heterogeneity is exactly why the paper's scope is \emph{consumer-facing,
limited-reclaim firms}. Setting $\omega\to0$ and $\beta\to1$ in~\eqref{eq:liu-net}
recovers the all-B2C, no-reclaim special case in which registration is an unambiguous
net cost equal to the full output VAT, and the single-wedge, \citet{klevenwaseem2013}
treatment of the notch applies cleanly; this is the population the
turnover-tax-notch model of Section~\ref{sec:model} is built to describe (the
``Scope: a turnover-tax notch'' paragraph there). Away from that corner the sign of
the registration incentive is firm-specific, and a full $\omega$/$\beta$-heterogeneous
costing---which would price voluntary registration and the B2B/B2C split alongside
bunching---is left for future work, once $\omega$ and $\beta$ can be measured from
ONS Supply--Use data.

This section completes the costing framework. Where Section~\ref{sec:static} prices
each reform mechanically and Section~\ref{sec:model} measures the distortion it
removes, the simulator here prices the \emph{level and rate} reforms
\emph{behaviourally}: it lets firms re-optimise value added as the effective rate
changes and implies that, for any assumed $e$, the base-broadening response would
offset a fraction of the static revenue loss. The layer is bounded by what it can
and cannot identify. The behavioural magnitudes are conditional on an assumed,
non-identified $e$, and so are a sensitivity range rather than forecasts; the
priced margin is purely \emph{intensive}, since the model re-scales value added while
holding the extensive bunching response fixed; and the registration incentive is
treated as a single wedge, abstracting from the input-reclaim and B2C heterogeneity
of \citet{liuetal2021} that the previous subsection sets out. Crucially, the
graduated taper's behavioural response is \emph{not} credibly identified in this
framework: its base-broadening and marginal-rate channels pull in opposite directions
and the iso-elastic cost cannot net them, so I price it only statically and through
its exact dominated-region removal. The robust anchors of the paper therefore remain
the $e$-free static cost of Section~\ref{sec:static} and the exact dominated region
of Section~\ref{sec:model}; the contribution here is a disciplined, transparent layer
that prices the intensive behavioural response to the level and rate reforms once one
is willing to name an elasticity.
