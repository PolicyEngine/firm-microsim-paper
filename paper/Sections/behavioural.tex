\section{Behavioural reform costs: registration heterogeneity}
\label{sec:behavioural}

The reforms of Section~\ref{sec:static} are costed \emph{statically}: each schedule
is re-applied to a fixed turnover distribution, and the revenue change is the
mechanical reclassification of firms with no response in turnover.
Section~\ref{sec:bunching} then shows that the bunching in my synthetic data is
mechanical, so a behavioural elasticity is not identified from it---the reduced-form
response is a placebo. Section~\ref{sec:model} supplies the one object that needs no
behavioural calibration at all: the exact, elasticity-free dominated region. What is
missing from this sequence is the behavioural layer itself. In the UK VAT setting that
layer is, in the first instance, not an intensive turnover response but an
\emph{extensive} one: the decision of whether to register. I follow
\citet{liuetal2021}, who place the registration choice at the centre of the threshold
notch and document its empirical signature directly in the administrative data---most
strikingly, that roughly $43\%$ of UK firms below the threshold register
\emph{voluntarily}. This section sets out their structure in full and uses it to price
the threshold and rate reforms parametrically on the registration margin. Throughout, I
am explicit that the elasticity-free anchors of the paper remain the static cost of
Section~\ref{sec:static} and the exact dominated region of Section~\ref{sec:model};
the registration costing here is a transparent, composition-conditional sensitivity
layer that sits on top of them.

\subsection{The firm's problem: a registration model}
\label{ssec:liu-model}

I adopt the structural registration model of \citet{liuetal2021}, in the
sufficient-statistics tradition of \citet{saez2010} and \citet{klevenwaseem2013}.
Consider a small firm of productivity $a$ and input share $\omega\in[0,1]$ that sells
to two markets: final consumers (business-to-consumer, B2C, quantity $x$) and other
businesses (business-to-business, B2B, quantity $y$). Production is fixed-coefficients,
following \citet{keenmintz2004}: one unit of output requires $\omega/a$ units of a taxed
intermediate input and $(1-\omega)/a$ units of labour, with the input price and the wage
both normalised to one. Absent VAT the unit cost is therefore
$\omega/a + (1-\omega)/a = 1/a$, and $\omega$ is the share of costs that are
taxed, deductible inputs. The defining feature of VAT is that this input tax is
reclaimable only by a registered firm, so the firm's unit cost depends on its
registration status. Writing the VAT rate as $t$, a registered firm reclaims the input
VAT and so still pays $\omega/a$ for inputs, while an unregistered firm pays the
VAT-inclusive price $(\omega/a)(1+t)$; the unit cost is thus
\begin{equation}
  c \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{\omega}{a}+\dfrac{1-\omega}{a} \;=\; \dfrac{1}{a}, & \text{registered,}\\[2ex]
    \dfrac{\omega}{a}(1+t)+\dfrac{1-\omega}{a} \;=\; \dfrac{1+\omega t}{a}, & \text{unregistered.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:liu-cost}
\end{equation}
The gap $\omega t/a$ between the two is the irrecoverable input VAT an unregistered firm
bears---the reclaim benefit of registering, rising in the input share $\omega$, which is
the first of the two forces in \eqref{eq:liu-netbenefit} below.

Demand in each market is iso-elastic (CES). Let $I\in\{0,1\}$ be the registration
indicator, $e_C$ and $e_B$ the B2C and B2B demand elasticities, $A_C,A_B$ market scale
terms, and $\lambda$ the B2C taste weight. With $p_C,p_B$ the firm's pre-tax prices,
\begin{align}
  x &= \lambda A_C\,\bigl(p_C(1+It)\bigr)^{-e_C}, \notag\\
  y &= (1-\lambda)A_B\,p_B^{-e_B}.
  \label{eq:liu-demand}
\end{align}
Output VAT grosses up the price the buyer faces only in the B2C market, and only when
the firm is registered---this is the factor $1+It$ in the first line of
\eqref{eq:liu-demand}. In the B2B market the buyer reclaims any VAT charged, so output
VAT is neutral on that side and does not enter the second line. Let $\beta$ denote the
firm's B2C sales share. The firm sets a constant-elasticity monopoly markup over its
unit cost in each market,
\begin{equation}
  p_C \;=\; \frac{e_C}{e_C-1}\,c,
  \qquad
  p_B \;=\; \frac{e_B}{e_B-1}\,c,
  \label{eq:liu-prices}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the registration-dependent unit cost of \eqref{eq:liu-cost}. Because the
consumer pays the grossed-up price $p_C(1+t)$ once the firm registers, registration
simultaneously lowers the firm's unit cost (through input reclaim) and raises the
price consumers face (through output VAT), so it depresses B2C quantity even as it
expands the B2B margin. The firm chooses prices and the registration indicator $I$ to
maximise profit
\begin{equation}
  \pi \;=\; (p_C-c)\,x \;+\; (p_B-c)\,y,
  \label{eq:liu-profit}
\end{equation}
subject, if it remains unregistered ($I=0$), to keeping sales $p_C x + p_B y$ below the
registration threshold $s^{*}$.

\paragraph{Who gains from registering.} Comparing optimised profit in
\eqref{eq:liu-profit} across $I=1$ and $I=0$, the net benefit of registering is, to
first order, proportional to
\begin{equation}
  t\,\bigl[\,\omega \;-\; \beta(1-\omega)\,\bigr].
  \label{eq:liu-netbenefit}
\end{equation}
The two terms are the two faces of VAT. The reclaim benefit $t\,\omega$ rises in the
input share $\omega$: an input-heavy firm gains by recovering the VAT on its inputs.
The output burden $t\,\beta(1-\omega)$ rises in the B2C sales share $\beta$: a
consumer-facing firm loses, because the output VAT it cannot pass on falls on its B2C
sales. Hence \eqref{eq:liu-netbenefit} is \emph{negative} for consumer-facing,
low-input firms---for them the threshold is a downward notch and the model predicts
bunching just below it---and \emph{positive} for input-heavy, B2B-facing firms, who
register \emph{voluntarily} even below the threshold. This sign flip is the model's
central qualitative content, and it rationalises the administrative fact that some
$43\%$ of below-threshold UK firms register voluntarily \citep{liuetal2021}.
Figure~\ref{fig:liu_registration_choice} contrasts two representative firms---a
consumer-facing, low-input firm and an input-heavy, business-facing one---and

types under the hard notch and a graduated taper.

\subsection{Parametric budget cost on the registration margin}
\label{ssec:liu-costing}

This costing prices the \emph{extensive} margin only---whether each firm sits inside or
outside the VAT net---and holds turnover fixed. The \emph{intensive} response embedded in
the CES demand \eqref{eq:liu-demand}, by which registration depresses B2C quantity, is
part of the model but is not re-solved here; pricing it is the full behavioural exercise
left to future work. On the extensive margin, the registration decision
\eqref{eq:liu-netbenefit} has a direct, static implication for revenue. Consider the VAT
the government collects per firm. A \emph{registered} firm remits VAT on its value added,
$t(1-\omega)y$, since it charges output VAT and reclaims input VAT. An
\emph{unregistered} firm remits nothing directly, but the input VAT it cannot reclaim,
$t\,\omega\,y$, sticks and reaches the Exchequer as irrecoverable tax embedded in its
purchases. A firm is inside the VAT net if it must register, $y\ge T^{*}$, or chooses
to, $\omega-\beta(1-\omega)>0$. A reform that changes who is inside the net therefore
moves revenue per affected firm between $t(1-\omega)y$ (registered) and $t\,\omega\,y$
(unregistered)---a swing that depends entirely on whether $\omega$ is above or below
$\tfrac12$. Applying this accounting to the firm population, weighted by the assumed
composition $(\omega,\beta)$, yields a budget cost for each reform.
Table~\ref{tab:liu_costing} reports these population-weighted figures.

The key finding is that raising the threshold costs revenue \emph{only} for the
low-$\omega$, high-$\beta$ consumer-facing firms. These firms de-register when the
threshold rises, and because $\omega<\tfrac12$ for them they then yield less as
unregistered firms ($t\,\omega\,y$) than they did as registered firms ($t(1-\omega)y$),
so the Exchequer loses. Input-heavy firms, by contrast, register \emph{voluntarily}
under \eqref{eq:liu-netbenefit} regardless of where the threshold sits, so a change in
the threshold is irrelevant to them and the raise-to-\pounds100k column is zero for
every type with $\omega\ge\tfrac12$. Across the plausible range of $(\omega,\beta)$ the
raise-to-\pounds100k cost therefore ranges from \pounds0 to about $-\pounds1.05$bn. The
two reduced-rate bands, which lower the output-VAT burden on \emph{everyone} who would
otherwise be registered rather than only the de-registering margin, cost more uniformly
across firm types: up to about $-\pounds0.93$bn for the $10\%$ band and $-\pounds0.47$bn
for the $15\%$ band, with the population-average composition $(0.5,0.5)$ implying
$-\pounds0.58$bn and $-\pounds0.29$bn respectively. Figure~\ref{fig:liu_costing} plots
the reform cost against $\omega$ and the raise-to-\pounds100k cost surface over the full
$(\omega,\beta)$ plane.

\paragraph{Caveats and scope.} I am explicit about what this layer is and is not. It is
a \emph{static, extensive-margin} estimate: firms switch registration status, but their
turnover and prices are held fixed. It is \emph{not} the full behavioural response of
\citet{liuetal2021}, in which the CES demand system \eqref{eq:liu-demand} is re-solved
through the optimal prices \eqref{eq:liu-prices} so that prices, quantities, and the tax
base all adjust jointly; recovering that, together with grounding $\omega$ and $\beta$
in ONS Supply--Use data (including the final-demand split), is left for future work. The
treatment of B2B VAT cascading is simplified: I credit unregistered firms only with
their own irrecoverable input VAT and do not trace tax embedded further down the chain.
And the $(\omega,\beta)$ values are not data-grounded, so the figures in
Table~\ref{tab:liu_costing} are a sensitivity range rather than point estimates or a
forecast. This margin is exactly the ``turnover-tax notch'' corner of the model in
Section~\ref{sec:model}: in the limit $\omega\to0$, $\beta\to1$---an all-B2C firm with
no reclaimable inputs---the registration choice collapses to the pure downward notch of
\citet{klevenwaseem2013}, and the dominated region of Section~\ref{sec:model} is the
elasticity-free object that survives whatever one assumes about $(\omega,\beta)$. That
dominated region, and the static cost of Section~\ref{sec:static}, remain the paper's
robust anchors; the registration model adds a disciplined account of \emph{who} bears a
threshold reform's cost, conditional on a composition that future ONS work will
pin down.
